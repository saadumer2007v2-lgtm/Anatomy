    Body systems · Integration

    How the systems work together

    No system works alone. Follow one ordinary event and you touch almost every system, which is how clinical problems present too.

    You sprint for the bus

    Decide · NervousMotor cortex fires; signals run down the corticospinal tract to anterior horn cells, out through the lumbosacral plexus into the femoral and sciatic nerves.
    Contract · Muscular & skeletalAcetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction triggers contraction of the glutes, quadriceps and calf; bones act as levers at hip, knee and ankle.
    Alarm · Endocrine & autonomicSympathetic outflow and adrenaline from the adrenal medulla raise heart rate, dilate bronchi and release glucose.
    Breathe · RespiratoryRate and depth rise to load oxygen and unload carbon dioxide.
    Deliver · CardiovascularCardiac output rises; arterioles in working muscle dilate while gut vessels constrict. The calf muscle pump pushes venous blood back up.
    Cool · IntegumentarySkin vessels dilate and sweat glands fire to shed heat.
    Recover · Renal, lymphatic, GIKidneys correct water and acid; lymphatics drain extra tissue fluid; the liver recycles lactate.

    Levels of organisation

    Cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism. A femur is an organ: bone tissue, cartilage, marrow, blood vessels and nerves working together.

    The four tissue types

    • Epithelial: covers and lines (epidermis, gut lining).
    • Connective: supports and connects (bone, cartilage, tendon, ligament, fascia, fat, blood).
    • Muscle: skeletal (voluntary, striated), cardiac (striated, involuntary), smooth (involuntary).
    • Nervous: neurons that signal and glia that support them.
    Body systems · Terminology

    The language of anatomy

    Every description assumes the anatomical position: standing upright, facing forward, arms by the sides with palms facing forward, feet together and pointing forward. "Medial" always means towards the midline of that position, whatever the patient is actually doing.

    Planes

    • Sagittal: splits left from right. The median (midsagittal) plane runs down the midline.
    • Coronal (frontal): splits front from back.
    • Transverse (axial): splits top from bottom. CT and MRI axial slices are viewed as if from the patient's feet, so the patient's right is on your left.

    Limb-specific words

    • Proximal / distal: nearer to / further from the limb's attachment to the trunk.
    • Palmar (volar) is the front of the hand; dorsal is the back of the hand and the top of the foot; plantar is the sole.
    • Radial / ulnar and tibial / fibular replace lateral / medial in the forearm and leg.

    Why the knee faces forward

    In the embryo both limb buds point sideways with thumbs and big toes facing up. Around week 7 the arm rotates 90° laterally, so the elbow points back and the thumb ends up lateral. The leg rotates 90° medially, so the knee points forward and the big toe ends up medial. That twist is why the leg's extensor compartment is at the front and why lower limb dermatomes spiral like a barber's pole.

    Six rules that do half the work
    1. One compartment, one nerve, one job. Learn the exceptions (FCU and medial FDP are ulnar; the hamstring part of adductor magnus is tibial).
    2. Posterior divisions supply extensors. Radial and axillary nerves in the arm; femoral, common fibular and gluteal nerves in the leg.
    3. Arteries change name at landmarks, not at branches: 1st rib, teres major, inguinal ligament, adductor hiatus.
    4. Four nerves hug bone: axillary (surgical neck of humerus), radial (radial groove), ulnar (medial epicondyle), common fibular (neck of fibula). Fracture there, test that nerve.
    5. Three bones have back-to-front blood supply: scaphoid, femoral head, talus. Fractures risk avascular necrosis.
    6. Superficial veins go up the preaxial and postaxial borders: cephalic and great saphenous on the thumb or big-toe side, basilic and small saphenous on the opposite side.
    Chapter 2 · Upper limb

    Upper limb

    The arm is built for reach and precision. A shallow shoulder gives a huge range of movement, the forearm turns the hand over, and the hand is run by five nerves that you can test in under a minute.

    The whole limb on one card

    • Regions: shoulder girdle and axilla → arm → elbow and cubital fossa → forearm → wrist and carpal tunnel → hand.
    • Nerves: brachial plexus C5–T1 gives five terminal nerves: musculocutaneous, axillary, radial, median, ulnar.
    • Artery line: subclavian → axillary (1st rib) → brachial (teres major) → radial + ulnar (cubital fossa) → palmar arches.
    • Veins: superficial cephalic and basilic, joined by the median cubital vein; deep veins travel in pairs beside the arteries.
    • Lymph: almost all drains to the axillary nodes.
    Think of it as

    A crane. The scapula is the swivelling base, the humerus and forearm are the boom, and the hand is the grab. The rotator cuff is the set of guy-ropes holding the boom onto a very shallow base plate.

    Upper limb · Bones

    Bones and their landmarks

    Every bump, groove and fossa on a bone is there for a reason: a muscle pulls on it, a joint surface sits on it, or a nerve or vessel runs past it. Tap a number or a name to see what each landmark does. Use Hide labels to test yourself.

    Carpal bones in order

    Lateral to medial, proximal row then distal row: Scaphoid, Lunate, Triquetrum, Pisiform; Trapezium, Trapezoid, Capitate, Hamate. "Some Lovers Try Positions That They Can't Handle." Trapezium sits under the thumb.

    Clinical link

    The scaphoid is the most commonly fractured carpal bone (fall on an outstretched hand, tender anatomical snuffbox). Its blood enters distally, so a waist fracture can starve the proximal pole: avascular necrosis. The lunate is the most commonly dislocated, and can press on the median nerve in the carpal tunnel.

    The shoulder is a golf ball balanced on a tee. The tee (glenoid) is tiny; the rotator cuff is the hand cupped around the ball. The hip, by contrast, is an egg sitting deep in an egg cup.

    Clinical link · shoulder dislocation

    Anterior dislocation: arm held slightly abducted and externally rotated, loss of the round shoulder contour. Associated lesions: Bankart (torn anterior labrum) and Hill–Sachs (dent in the posterior humeral head). Posterior dislocation is rare, follows seizures or electric shock, and shows the "lightbulb" humeral head on an AP X-ray.

    Mnemonics that earn their place
    • Rotator cuff, SITS: Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus, Teres minor (greater tubercle, top to bottom) and Subscapularis (lesser tubercle).
    • Intertubercular groove, "a lady between two majors": pectoralis major on the lateral lip, latissimus dorsi in the floor, teres major on the medial lip.
    • Superficial forearm flexors, lateral to medial, "Pass Fail Pass Fail": pronator teres, FCR, palmaris longus, FCU.
    • Thenar muscles + 1st and 2nd lumbricals = median, "LOAF": Lumbricals 1–2, Opponens pollicis, Abductor pollicis brevis, Flexor pollicis brevis. Every other intrinsic hand muscle is ulnar.

    Refinement

    "Supraspinatus does the first 15°, deltoid the rest" is a useful exam simplification. EMG studies show both act from the start; supraspinatus mainly holds the humeral head in the glenoid so the deltoid has something stable to pull against. Above about 90°, the scapula must rotate upwards (trapezius and serratus anterior), which is why serratus palsy limits overhead reach.

    Upper limb · Nerves

    The brachial plexus

    Five spinal nerve roots (C5–T1) mix and re-sort so that each terminal nerve carries fibres from several roots. Tap a terminal nerve to trace its fibres back to the roots and see what it supplies.

    Roots → trunks → divisions → cords → branches

    "Real Teenagers Drink Cold Beer". Right plexus, schematic.
    Tap Musculocutaneous, Axillary, Radial, Median or Ulnar.

    Where each part sits

    • Roots emerge between scalenus anterior and medius in the neck.
    • Trunks cross the posterior triangle of the neck, above the clavicle.
    • Divisions lie behind the clavicle.
    • Cords are in the axilla, named by their position around the 2nd part of the axillary artery: lateral, posterior, medial.
    • Terminal branches leave at the lower border of pectoralis minor.

    Branches before the terminal five

    • Roots: dorsal scapular C5 (rhomboids, levator scapulae); long thoracic C5–7 (serratus anterior). "C5, 6, 7 raise your wings to heaven."
    • Upper trunk: suprascapular C5–6 (supraspinatus, infraspinatus); nerve to subclavius C5–6.
    • Lateral cord: lateral pectoral C5–7.
    • Posterior cord, "ULTRA": Upper subscapular, Lower subscapular, Thoracodorsal (latissimus dorsi), then Radial and Axillary.
    • Medial cord: medial pectoral C8–T1; medial cutaneous nerves of arm and forearm.
    Think of it as

    A motorway interchange. Five roads come in (roots) and five leave (terminal nerves), but every exit road carries traffic from several entry roads. Damage one entry road and traffic slows on several exits: a single root lesion causes partial weakness in several muscles, while a single peripheral nerve lesion causes complete weakness in a few.

    Clinical link · plexus injuries

    Erb's palsy C5–6: the head is forced away from the shoulder (shoulder dystocia, motorbike fall). Arm adducted and medially rotated, elbow extended, forearm pronated: the "waiter's tip". Klumpke's palsy C8–T1: the arm is pulled upwards (grabbing a branch while falling, breech delivery). Claw hand from loss of intrinsic hand muscles; check for Horner's syndrome, since T1 carries sympathetic fibres to the head. A Pancoast tumour at the lung apex can do the same.

    The ulnar paradox

    A lesion closer to the hand gives a worse-looking claw. At the wrist, FDP to the ring and little fingers still works, so it pulls the fingertips into flexion against paralysed lumbricals. At the elbow, that half of FDP is paralysed too, so there is less pull and less clawing.

    Sign check

    Froment's sign: pinching paper between thumb and index finger, the thumb IP joint flexes because FPL (median) substitutes for a weak adductor pollicis (ulnar). Tinel's: tapping over the carpal tunnel reproduces tingling. Phalen's: holding the wrists fully flexed for up to a minute reproduces symptoms.

    Reflex rhyme

    "One, two, buckle my shoe (S1–2 ankle); three, four, kick the door (L3–4 knee); five, six, pick up sticks (C5–6 biceps); seven, eight, lay them straight (C7–8 triceps)."

    Clinical link · UMN or LMN?

    Lower motor neurone lesions (root, plexus, peripheral nerve) cause wasting, fasciculation, low tone and reduced reflexes. Upper motor neurone lesions (brain, spinal cord) cause increased tone, brisk reflexes and an upgoing plantar, with little wasting. This distinction is the first question in every PNS exam.

    FY1 link · arterial blood gas

    The radial artery is the default ABG site because it is superficial, easy to compress, and the hand has a second supply through the ulnar artery and palmar arches. The modified Allen's test checks that the ulnar artery fills the hand before you puncture. The femoral artery is the fallback in shocked patients.

    Clinical link · supracondylar fracture

    The brachial artery lies right against the front of the distal humerus. In a displaced extension-type supracondylar fracture in a child, check the radial pulse, capillary refill and hand colour. Missed ischaemia can lead to Volkmann's ischaemic contracture: fibrosis and shortening of the forearm flexors.

    Think of it as

    Anastomoses around the shoulder (scapular anastomosis) and elbow (collateral and recurrent arteries) are ring roads. If the main road is slowly blocked, traffic re-routes; if it is suddenly cut, the ring road may be too small.

    For bloods, use the median cubital vein. For a cannula you want a straight, palpable vein away from a joint: the cephalic vein on the forearm or wrist, or veins on the dorsum of the hand. Avoid a limb with a fistula, lymphoedema or on the side of an axillary node clearance.

    Clinical link

    Upper limb DVT is uncommon but happens with central lines, PICC lines and, in young people, repetitive overhead activity compressing the subclavian vein (Paget–Schroetter syndrome). Superficial thrombophlebitis at old cannula sites is very common.

    Upper limb · Lymphatics

    Lymphatic drainage

    Superficial lymph vessels follow the superficial veins; deep vessels follow the deep veins. Nearly everything ends up in the axillary nodes.

    Lymph pathway

    From hand to bloodstream.

    Axillary node groups (about 20–30 nodes)

    • Pectoral (anterior): along the lateral thoracic vessels; receive most lymph from the breast.
    • Humeral (lateral): along the axillary vein; receive most lymph from the arm.
    • Subscapular (posterior): posterior axillary wall, back of the chest and shoulder.
    • Central: in the axillary fat; receive from the three groups above.
    • Apical: at the apex; receive from all groups and the cephalic-side vessels, then drain into the subclavian lymph trunk.

    Supratrochlear (cubital) nodes sit above the medial epicondyle and receive lymph from the medial forearm and hand.

    Clinical link · breast cancer

    About three-quarters of breast lymph drains to the axillary nodes, so breast cancer spreads there first. Sentinel node biopsy samples the first node; a full axillary clearance risks arm lymphoedema and injury to the long thoracic nerve (winged scapula) and thoracodorsal nerve (weak latissimus dorsi).

    Clinical link · infection

    An infected finger can cause red streaks up the arm (lymphangitis) and tender axillary or supratrochlear nodes. These need prompt antibiotics.

    Upper limb · Soft tissues

    Fascia, compartments, bursae and tendons

    Deep fascia wraps the limb like cling film and sends septa down to the bone, dividing muscles into compartments. Bursae are friction-reducing fluid sacs; retinacula are straps that stop tendons bowstringing.

    Fascia and compartments

    • Arm: brachial fascia with medial and lateral intermuscular septa → anterior compartment (flexors, musculocutaneous) and posterior compartment (triceps, radial).
    • Forearm: antebrachial fascia, interosseous membrane and radius/ulna → anterior (flexor-pronator) and posterior (extensor-supinator) compartments.
    • Bicipital aponeurosis: a fascial band from the biceps tendon to the forearm fascia; protects the brachial artery and median nerve during venepuncture.
    • Clavipectoral fascia: encloses subclavius and pectoralis minor; pierced by the cephalic vein.
    • Axillary sheath: fascia from the neck wrapping the axillary artery and plexus; local anaesthetic injected here gives an axillary plexus block.
    • Palmar aponeurosis: tough triangular fascia in the palm. Thickening and contracture is Dupuytren's disease (ring and little fingers pulled into flexion).
    • Retinacula: flexor retinaculum (roof of the carpal tunnel) and extensor retinaculum (six tunnels for extensor tendons).

    Bursae

    • Subacromial (subdeltoid) bursa: between the acromion/deltoid and the supraspinatus tendon. Inflamed in subacromial impingement: painful arc between about 60° and 120° of abduction.
    • Subscapular bursa: between subscapularis and the scapula; usually communicates with the shoulder joint.
    • Olecranon bursa: over the point of the elbow. Swells with repeated leaning ("student's elbow"), gout or infection. A hot, red, tender bursa needs aspiration to exclude septic bursitis.

    Tendons that cause trouble

    • Supraspinatus: squeezed under the acromion; the most commonly torn rotator cuff tendon.
    • Long head of biceps: runs in the intertubercular groove inside the joint; rupture gives a "Popeye" bulge.
    • Common extensor origin (lateral epicondyle): tennis elbow, pain on resisted wrist extension.
    • Common flexor origin (medial epicondyle): golfer's elbow, pain on resisted wrist flexion.
    • APL and EPB in the first extensor compartment: De Quervain's tenosynovitis (Finkelstein's test).
    • Extensor tendon at the DIP joint: mallet finger after a ball strikes the fingertip.
    • Flexor tendon sheath: trigger finger; infected sheath (flexor tenosynovitis) is a surgical emergency.
    • EPL: hooks around Lister's tubercle; can rupture weeks after a distal radius fracture.
    Sprain or strain?

    A sprain is a ligament injury. A strain is an injury to a muscle or the muscle–tendon junction. Tendon injuries themselves are usually called tendinopathy or rupture.

    Clinically important regions

    Spaces, tunnels and fossae where several important structures squeeze through a small gap. These are where you take blood, feel pulses, and where nerves get trapped.

    Shape

    Cubital fossa

    Borders

    Carpal tunnel and Guyon's canal

    Borders

    Inject into the bulk of the deltoid, roughly 2.5–5 cm below the acromion, in the middle of the muscle. Too low or too far back risks the radial nerve in the radial groove; too high hits the subacromial bursa or acromion. The axillary nerve runs around the surgical neck, below the safe zone.

    Imaging tips · upper limb X-rays

    Always ask for two views ("one view is no view"). Elbow: a raised anterior fat pad (sail sign) or any visible posterior fat pad means an effusion, which implies an occult fracture (supracondylar in children, radial head in adults). The anterior humeral line should pass through the middle third of the capitellum; the radiocapitellar line should hit the capitellum in every view. Scaphoid: request dedicated scaphoid views.

    FY1 link · neurovascular check

    Before and after any manipulation, splint or cast, document: radial pulse, capillary refill, and the motor and sensory function of the radial (thumbs up, first dorsal web space), median (OK sign, index pulp, thumb abduction) and ulnar nerves (finger spread, little finger pulp). It takes 30 seconds and protects your patient and you.

    Clinical link · compartment syndrome of the forearm

    After a crush injury, fracture or tight cast, rising pressure inside a fascial compartment cuts off perfusion. The earliest and most reliable signs are pain out of proportion to the injury and pain on passive stretch of the muscles (extending the fingers). Pulses usually remain present until very late. It is a surgical emergency needing fasciotomy.

    Chapter 3 · Lower limb

    Lower limb

    The leg is built for weight-bearing and propulsion. A deep hip, a locking knee and a sprung foot carry your whole body weight with every step, and that load explains most of its anatomy and most of its injuries.

    The whole limb on one card

    • Regions: gluteal region → thigh (anterior, medial, posterior compartments) → knee and popliteal fossa → leg (anterior, lateral, posterior compartments) → ankle → foot.
    • Nerves: lumbar plexus L1–4 (femoral, obturator) and sacral plexus L4–S4 (sciatic → tibial + common fibular, gluteal nerves).
    • Artery line: external iliac → femoral (inguinal ligament) → popliteal (adductor hiatus) → anterior tibial + tibioperoneal trunk → posterior tibial and fibular.
    • Veins: great and small saphenous superficially; deep veins beside the arteries; perforators link the two.
    • Lymph: superficial and deep inguinal nodes, with a small popliteal group.
    Think of it as

    A pillar standing on a spring. The femur and tibia are the pillar, the knee locks it straight so you can stand without effort, and the arches of the foot are leaf springs that store energy at each step and give it back at push-off.

    Locking and unlocking the knee

    In the last few degrees of extension with the foot planted, the femur rotates medially on the tibia and the ligaments tighten: the knee "screws home" and locks, so you can stand with almost no quadriceps effort. To bend the knee again, popliteus rotates the femur laterally and unlocks it.

    Arches of the foot

    • Medial longitudinal (highest): calcaneus, talus, navicular, cuneiforms, medial three metatarsals. Supported by tibialis posterior, FHL, the spring ligament and the plantar aponeurosis.
    • Lateral longitudinal (low): calcaneus, cuboid, lateral two metatarsals. Long and short plantar ligaments, fibularis longus.
    • Transverse: cuneiforms, cuboid and metatarsal bases. Fibularis longus and tibialis posterior form a stirrup under it.

    Flat foot (pes planus) often reflects tibialis posterior dysfunction; a high arch (pes cavus) can signal a neuropathy such as Charcot–Marie–Tooth.

    Think of it as

    The windlass mechanism: the plantar aponeurosis is a cable from heel to toes. When the big toe bends up at push-off, the cable winds around the metatarsal head like a rope on a winch, shortening it and lifting the arch into a rigid lever.

    Clinical tests

    ACL: Lachman's test (most sensitive) and anterior drawer. PCL: posterior drawer and posterior sag. MCL / LCL: valgus / varus stress at 0° and 30° of flexion. Menisci: joint line tenderness and McMurray's test. The "unhappy triad" (ACL, MCL, medial meniscus) follows a blow to the outside of the knee; more recent studies show the lateral meniscus is actually torn more often in acute ACL injuries.

    Lower limb · Nerves

    The lumbosacral plexus

    Two plexuses joined by the lumbosacral trunk. The lumbar plexus forms inside psoas major and supplies the front and inner thigh; the sacral plexus lies on piriformis and supplies the buttock, back of thigh, and everything below the knee except a strip of medial skin.

    Branching flowchart

    Tap a nerve for its roots, supply and what an injury looks like.

    Lumbar plexus: where the nerves come out of psoas

    • Lateral border of psoas: iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal, lateral femoral cutaneous, femoral.
    • Front of psoas: genitofemoral.
    • Medial border of psoas: obturator (and the lumbosacral trunk).

    Root pattern: "2 from 1, 2 from 2, 2 from 3". Iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal from L1; genitofemoral and lateral femoral cutaneous from two roots each (L1–2, L2–3); obturator and femoral from three roots each (L2–4).

    Sacral plexus: leaving the pelvis

    • Above piriformis through the greater sciatic foramen: superior gluteal nerve and vessels.
    • Below piriformis: sciatic, inferior gluteal, posterior femoral cutaneous and pudendal nerves, plus the nerves to obturator internus and quadratus femoris.
    • Pudendal hooks around the sacrospinous ligament and re-enters through the lesser sciatic foramen to reach the perineum ("S2, 3, 4 keeps the poo off the floor").

    Loss: foot drop, high-stepping gait, weak eversion, numb dorsum of foot and lateral leg. Inversion is preserved.

    Gluteal nerves L4–S2

    1. Superior gluteal L4–S1Above piriformis, between gluteus medius and minimus; supplies both plus TFL.Injection in the wrong quadrant · hip surgery
    2. Inferior gluteal L5–S2Below piriformis; supplies gluteus maximus only.

    Superior gluteal loss: positive Trendelenburg sign and gait. Inferior gluteal loss: difficulty climbing stairs or rising from a chair.

    Trendelenburg sign: get the side right

    Stand on the right leg. The right gluteus medius and minimus contract to keep the pelvis level. If the pelvis drops on the left (the unsupported side), the problem is on the right (the standing side): right superior gluteal nerve, right abductor weakness, or right hip pathology.

    Which root does a disc hit?

    Lumbar roots exit below their own vertebra. A typical posterolateral disc prolapse at L4/5 misses the L4 root (already leaving higher in its foramen) and compresses the L5 root travelling down to exit at L5/S1. So L4/5 disc → L5 root; L5/S1 disc → S1 root. The straight leg raise reproduces leg pain when a lower root is stretched over a disc.

    Emergency · cauda equina syndrome

    Compression of the bundle of lumbosacral roots below the end of the spinal cord. Red flags: bilateral sciatica, severe or progressive bilateral leg weakness, new difficulty starting to pass urine or reduced sensation of urine flow, loss of rectal fullness sensation, saddle (perianal, perineal, genital) numbness, new sexual dysfunction. The national GIRFT pathway asks for an emergency MRI within 4 hours of request. Painless urinary retention with overflow is a late sign: do not wait for it.

    Lower limb · Arteries

    Arterial supply

    One trunk that changes name at the inguinal ligament and the adductor hiatus, then splits into three below the knee. Tap any vessel for details.

    Branching flowchart

    Scroll sideways on a phone.

    The main line where the name changes

    1. Abdominal aortaDivides into common iliac arteries at L4, about the level of the umbilicus.
    2. Common iliac → internal + external iliacSplits in front of the sacroiliac joint. Internal iliac supplies the pelvis and buttock (gluteal arteries, obturator artery).
    3. Inguinal ligament → femoral arteryEnters the thigh at the mid-inguinal point; gives profunda femoris, the main supply of the thigh.
    4. Adductor hiatus → popliteal arteryPasses through the gap in adductor magnus into the popliteal fossa, the deepest structure there.
    5. Lower border of popliteus → anterior tibial + tibioperoneal trunkThe trunk splits into posterior tibial and fibular arteries.
    6. FootAnterior tibial becomes dorsalis pedis at the ankle; posterior tibial divides into medial and lateral plantar arteries; the plantar arch joins the two systems.

    Pulses to palpate

    • Femoral: mid-inguinal point, halfway between the ASIS and the pubic symphysis.
    • Popliteal: deep in the fossa; flex the knee slightly so the fascia relaxes and press firmly with both hands.
    • Posterior tibial: behind the medial malleolus, halfway to the Achilles tendon.
    • Dorsalis pedis: on the dorsum of the foot, just lateral to the extensor hallucis longus tendon, over the bases of the 1st and 2nd metatarsals. Congenitally absent in a minority of people.

    Do not confuse the mid-inguinal point (ASIS to pubic symphysis: femoral artery) with the midpoint of the inguinal ligament (ASIS to pubic tubercle: deep inguinal ring).

    Clinical link · peripheral arterial disease

    Atherosclerosis narrows the arteries; pain comes from the muscles just below the blockage. Calf claudication suggests femoropopliteal disease (often near the adductor hiatus); buttock and thigh claudication suggests aortoiliac disease. An ankle–brachial pressure index below 0.9 supports PAD; a very high value suggests stiff, calcified vessels (diabetes, kidney disease).

    Emergency · acute limb ischaemia

    The six Ps: pain, pallor, pulselessness, perishing cold, paraesthesia and paralysis. Sensory and motor loss mean the limb is threatened and needs urgent vascular surgery.

    Clinical link · femoral head blood supply

    Most blood reaches the femoral head through retinacular branches of the medial circumflex femoral artery (from profunda femoris), running up along the neck under the capsule. A displaced intracapsular neck of femur fracture tears them, so the head is at high risk of avascular necrosis. The artery in the ligament of the head matters mainly in children.

    Lower limb · Veins

    Venous drainage

    Blood has to climb over a metre against gravity. One-way valves and the squeeze of the calf muscles do it. When valves or muscles fail, you get varicose veins, swelling and clots.

    Drainage flowchart

    Blood flows left to right, from foot to heart.

    Great saphenous vein longest vein in the body

    1. Dorsal venous archStarts on the medial side of the dorsum of the foot.
    2. AnklePasses in front of the medial malleolus with the saphenous nerve: reliable for a cut-down cannula in an emergency.
    3. KneePasses behind the medial femoral condyle.
    4. ThighClimbs the medial thigh.
    5. Saphenous openingPierces the cribriform fascia about 3–4 cm below and lateral to the pubic tubercle to join the femoral vein (saphenofemoral junction).

    Harvested for coronary artery bypass grafts.

    Small saphenous vein

    1. Dorsal venous archStarts on the lateral side of the foot.
    2. AnklePasses behind the lateral malleolus with the sural nerve.
    3. CalfClimbs the midline of the back of the calf.
    4. Popliteal fossaPierces the deep fascia to join the popliteal vein (saphenopopliteal junction; the level varies).

    Deep veins

    1. LegPaired veins beside the anterior tibial, posterior tibial and fibular arteries, running within the calf muscles (the soleal sinuses).
    2. Popliteal veinFormed behind the knee; superficial to the popliteal artery.
    3. Femoral veinFrom the adductor hiatus; joined by the profunda femoris vein and great saphenous vein.
    4. External iliac veinFrom the inguinal ligament → common iliac → inferior vena cava (from about L5).

    The "superficial femoral vein" is a deep vein despite its old name. A clot there is a proximal DVT.

    Think of it as

    The calf muscle pump is a second heart. Each step squeezes the deep veins inside the soleus like a toothpaste tube, and the valves only let blood go upwards and from superficial to deep through the perforators. Lying still in a hospital bed switches that pump off.

    FY1 link · suspected DVT

    Virchow's triad: stasis, vessel wall injury, hypercoagulability. NICE NG158 uses the two-level DVT Wells score. If DVT is "likely", arrange a proximal leg vein ultrasound within 4 hours (if not possible, do a D-dimer and give interim anticoagulation). If "unlikely", do a D-dimer and scan only if it is positive. A ruptured Baker's cyst can mimic DVT almost exactly.

    Clinical link · varicose veins

    Valves fail, most often at the saphenofemoral junction, so blood refluxes from deep to superficial veins, which dilate. Complications include skin changes (haemosiderin, eczema, lipodermatosclerosis), venous ulcers over the medial malleolus (gaiter area) and superficial thrombophlebitis.

    Lower limb · Lymphatics

    Lymphatic drainage

    Superficial vessels follow the saphenous veins; deep vessels follow the deep veins. Everything ends in the deep inguinal nodes and then climbs to the thoracic duct.

    Lymph pathway

    From foot to bloodstream.

    Inguinal node groups

    • Superficial inguinal, horizontal group: just below the inguinal ligament. Drains the lower abdominal wall below the umbilicus, buttock, perineum, external genitalia (not the testes), the anal canal below the pectinate line and the lower vagina.
    • Superficial inguinal, vertical group: along the end of the great saphenous vein. Drains most of the superficial leg.
    • Deep inguinal: 1–3 nodes medial to the femoral vein; the highest sits in the femoral canal (Cloquet's node). Receives the superficial nodes, the popliteal nodes and deep leg lymph.
    • Popliteal: in the popliteal fat. Drains the lateral foot and heel (following the small saphenous vein) and deep structures of the leg.
    Clinical link · a lump in the groin

    Enlarged inguinal nodes can come from anything they drain: foot or leg infection, sexually transmitted infections, melanoma of the leg, and cancers of the vulva, penis or anal canal. Testicular cancer spreads to the para-aortic nodes instead, because the testes develop in the abdomen and keep their lymphatics there.

    Clinical link · lymphoedema and cellulitis

    Lymphoedema after groin node surgery or radiotherapy causes non-pitting swelling and repeated cellulitis. Cellulitis itself spreads as a hot, red, tender area, sometimes with red streaks and tender groin nodes.

    Lower limb · Soft tissues

    Fascia, compartments, bursae and tendons

    The leg's deep fascia is especially tight, which makes compartment syndrome far more common in the leg than the arm.

    Fascia and compartments

    • Fascia lata: the deep fascia of the thigh. Thickened laterally into the iliotibial tract (from the iliac crest, TFL and gluteus maximus to Gerdy's tubercle on the tibia). The saphenous opening lets the great saphenous vein through.
    • Thigh: three intermuscular septa give anterior (femoral nerve), medial (obturator) and posterior (sciatic) compartments.
    • Leg: crural fascia, interosseous membrane and septa give four compartments: anterior (deep fibular), lateral (superficial fibular), superficial posterior and deep posterior (tibial).
    • Retinacula at the ankle: superior and inferior extensor retinacula (front), flexor retinaculum (roof of the tarsal tunnel), superior and inferior fibular retinacula (hold the fibular tendons behind the lateral malleolus).
    • Plantar aponeurosis: from the medial calcaneal tubercle to the toes. Plantar fasciitis gives heel pain worst on the first steps in the morning.

    Bursae

    • Prepatellar: in front of the patella. Kneeling upright: "housemaid's knee".
    • Superficial infrapatellar: in front of the patellar tendon: "clergyman's knee".
    • Suprapatellar: above the patella and continuous with the knee joint, so an effusion fills it (the basis of the patellar tap).
    • Pes anserine: under the pes anserinus; medial knee pain below the joint line.
    • Semimembranosus–gastrocnemius: can swell into a Baker's (popliteal) cyst, usually with knee arthritis. Rupture mimics DVT.
    • Trochanteric: over the greater trochanter. Lateral hip pain, worse lying on that side (greater trochanteric pain syndrome).
    • Retrocalcaneal: between the Achilles tendon and calcaneus.

    Tendons that cause trouble

    • Achilles (calcaneal): the strongest tendon. Rupture feels like being kicked in the calf; Simmonds' (Thompson) test: squeezing the calf fails to plantarflex the foot. Risk rises with fluoroquinolones and steroids.
    • Patellar tendon: jumper's knee. In adolescents, traction on the tibial tuberosity causes Osgood–Schlatter disease.
    • Quadriceps tendon: rupture in older adults; cannot straight-leg raise.
    • Tibialis posterior: failure flattens the medial arch ("too many toes" seen from behind).
    • Fibularis brevis: can avulse the base of the 5th metatarsal in an inversion injury.
    • Iliotibial band: friction over the lateral femoral epicondyle in runners.
    Emergency · compartment syndrome of the leg

    Most often follows a tibial shaft fracture and usually affects the anterior compartment. Early signs: pain out of proportion to the injury and pain on passive stretch (pointing the toes stretches the anterior compartment); numbness in the first web space (deep fibular nerve) is an early nerve sign. Pulses are usually present. The BOA standard (BOAST, updated 2025): release casts and dressings, reassess, measure compartment pressure if the picture is unclear (a gap between diastolic pressure and compartment pressure under 30 mmHg is concerning), and decompress by fasciotomy as soon as the diagnosis is made.

    Lower limb · Clinical regions

    Clinically important regions

    Where you feel pulses, give injections, place lines and find hernias.

    Gluteal region and safe injection sites

    Danger
    The sciatic nerve runs through the lower medial and lower lateral quadrants, midway between the greater trochanter and ischial tuberosity.
    Safer sites
    The ventrogluteal site (gluteus medius, in the triangle between the greater trochanter, ASIS and iliac crest) is the preferred gluteal site, well away from the sciatic nerve. If the dorsogluteal site is used, inject only into the upper outer part of the upper outer quadrant. The superior gluteal nerve is still at risk from careless injections.
    Foramina
    Greater sciatic foramen: superior gluteal nerve and vessels above piriformis; sciatic, inferior gluteal, pudendal and posterior femoral cutaneous nerves below. Lesser sciatic foramen, "PIN": Pudendal nerve, Internal pudendal vessels, Nerve to obturator internus (plus its tendon).

    Femoral triangle and femoral canal

    Borders
    Superior: inguinal ligament. Lateral: medial border of sartorius. Medial: medial border of adductor longus. Floor: iliopsoas, pectineus, adductor longus. Roof: fascia lata with the saphenous opening.
    Contents
    Lateral to medial, NAVEL: femoral Nerve, femoral Artery, femoral Vein, Empty space (femoral canal), Lymphatics (deep inguinal nodes). The femoral sheath wraps the artery, vein and canal but not the nerve.
    Femoral canal
    Medial compartment of the sheath. Borders: inguinal ligament (front), pectineal ligament (back), lacunar ligament (medial), femoral vein (lateral). A femoral hernia appears below and lateral to the pubic tubercle, is commoner in women, and has a narrow neck, so it strangulates easily.
    Clinical
    Femoral pulse and arterial puncture; femoral venous access in emergencies (vein is medial to the artery); fascia iliaca or femoral nerve block for hip fracture pain; peripheral angiography. Radial access is now the default for coronary angiography in the UK.

    Popliteal fossa

    Borders
    A diamond. Superomedial: semimembranosus (and semitendinosus). Superolateral: biceps femoris. Inferomedial and inferolateral: medial and lateral heads of gastrocnemius.
    Floor / roof
    Floor: popliteal surface of the femur, back of the knee capsule, popliteus. Roof: popliteal fascia and skin; the small saphenous vein pierces it.
    Contents
    Superficial to deep: tibial nerve, popliteal vein, popliteal artery (deepest, which is why the pulse is hard to feel). The common fibular nerve follows biceps femoris laterally. Also popliteal lymph nodes and the end of the small saphenous vein.
    Clinical
    Popliteal aneurysm is the commonest peripheral aneurysm, often bilateral and linked with abdominal aortic aneurysm. Knee dislocation and supracondylar femur fractures can tear the popliteal artery. Baker's cyst lies on the medial side.

    Tarsal tunnel and the ankle

    Position
    Behind and below the medial malleolus, under the flexor retinaculum (which runs from the medial malleolus to the calcaneus).
    Contents
    Front to back, "Tom, Dick And Very Nervous Harry": Tibialis posterior, flexor Digitorum longus, posterior tibial Artery and Veins, tibial Nerve, flexor Hallucis longus.
    Behind the lateral malleolus
    Fibularis longus and brevis tendons, sural nerve, small saphenous vein.
    Clinical
    Posterior tibial pulse here. Tarsal tunnel syndrome: burning and numbness of the sole, positive Tinel's sign behind the medial malleolus.

    Adductor canal

    Course
    From the apex of the femoral triangle to the adductor hiatus (a gap in adductor magnus) in the lower thigh.
    Walls
    Roof (anteromedial): sartorius. Lateral: vastus medialis. Posterior: adductor longus and magnus.
    Contents
    Femoral artery, femoral vein, saphenous nerve, nerve to vastus medialis.
    Clinical
    Adductor canal block numbs the saphenous nerve for knee surgery while sparing most quadriceps power. Atherosclerosis often narrows the femoral artery near the hiatus, causing calf claudication.
    Lower limb · Clinical and FY1

    Clinical links and FY1 practice

    The lower limb fills orthopaedic, ED and elderly-care wards. These are the presentations you will clerk.

    Hip fracture (neck of femur)

    • Picture: older person after a fall, cannot weight-bear, leg shortened and externally rotated.
    • Anatomy: intracapsular fractures (subcapital, transcervical) tear the retinacular vessels; extracapsular fractures (intertrochanteric, subtrochanteric) spare them.
    • NICE CG124: surgery on the day of, or the day after, admission. Displaced intracapsular → replacement arthroplasty (hemiarthroplasty or total hip replacement for active, independent patients). Trochanteric → extramedullary implant such as a sliding hip screw. Subtrochanteric → intramedullary nail.
    • FY1 jobs: analgesia including a nerve block, bloods and group-and-save, ECG, correct anticoagulation, falls and bone-health assessment, orthogeriatric review.

    X-ray: Shenton's line (the curve along the femoral neck and the top of the obturator foramen) should be smooth; a step suggests a fracture.

    Knee injuries

    • ACL: non-contact pivot, "pop", swelling within hours, instability. Lachman's test.
    • Meniscus: twist on a flexed knee, swelling next day, locking, joint-line tenderness, McMurray's test.
    • MCL: blow to the outside of the knee; tender medially; valgus laxity.
    • Patellar dislocation: usually lateral, in young people; the medial patellofemoral ligament tears.
    • Ottawa knee rules for X-ray: age 55 or over, isolated patellar tenderness, fibular head tenderness, cannot flex to 90°, or cannot bear weight for 4 steps.

    A fat–fluid level on a horizontal-beam lateral X-ray (lipohaemarthrosis) means an intra-articular fracture.

    Ankle and foot injuries

    • Ottawa ankle rules: X-ray if there is malleolar pain and either bone tenderness along the posterior edge or tip of either malleolus (distal 6 cm), or inability to bear weight for 4 steps both immediately and in ED.
    • Ottawa foot rules: midfoot pain with tenderness at the base of the 5th metatarsal or the navicular, or inability to bear weight.
    • Weber classification of lateral malleolus fractures by level relative to the syndesmosis: A below (usually stable), B at, C above (unstable).
    • Maisonneuve fracture: a medial ankle injury with a high fibular fracture. Always feel the whole fibula.
    • 5th metatarsal base: avulsion by fibularis brevis versus a Jones fracture of the proximal shaft, which heals poorly.

    Foot drop: where is the lesion?

    • Common fibular nerve: eversion weak, inversion normal, numb dorsum of foot, reflexes normal, history of fibular neck trauma or a cast.
    • L5 root: inversion and hip abduction also weak, back pain radiating down the leg.
    • Sciatic nerve: plantarflexion and knee flexion weak too, ankle reflex lost.
    • Central causes (stroke, MS, motor neurone disease) have upper motor neurone signs.

    Management of a fibular palsy: ankle–foot orthosis, physiotherapy, remove the compression.

    FY1 link · intraosseous access

    When IV access fails in an arrest or shock, an intraosseous needle goes into the flat anteromedial surface of the proximal tibia, about a finger-breadth medial to the tibial tuberosity (just below it in children), or the proximal humerus. Avoid fractured bones.

    FY1 link · peripheral vascular examination

    Inspect for colour, hair loss, ulcers (arterial ulcers on toes and pressure points; venous ulcers in the gaiter area). Feel temperature and capillary refill, then the femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis pulses on both sides. Buerger's test and ABPI complete it.

    FY1 link · diabetic foot

    Diabetes damages nerves (loss of protective sensation, tested with a 10 g monofilament) and arteries. Ulcers form over pressure points such as the metatarsal heads and heel. Any hot, swollen diabetic foot needs urgent assessment for infection or Charcot neuroarthropathy.

    Imaging tips · lower limb X-rays

    Hip: AP pelvis plus a lateral of the affected hip; follow Shenton's line and the cortex of the femoral neck. If X-rays are normal but suspicion stays high, MRI. Knee: look for an effusion and lipohaemarthrosis. Ankle: AP, mortise and lateral views; the joint space around the talus should be even.

    Sources · Corrections

    Where the slides need fixing

    Peer slides are a good summary but contain errors that can cost marks. These are the ones worth correcting in your own notes and Anki cards.

    Upper limb deck · cubital fossa

    The slide is titled "cubital tunnel" but lists the contents of the cubital fossa (radial nerve, biceps tendon, brachial artery, median nerve; "brachial tendon" should read biceps tendon). The cubital tunnel is the ulnar nerve's passage behind the medial epicondyle.

    Upper limb deck · shoulder ligaments

    "Coracohumeral: conoid and trapezoid" is wrong. Conoid and trapezoid are the two parts of the coracoclavicular ligament. The coracohumeral ligament runs from the coracoid to the greater tubercle.

    Upper limb deck · Colles' fracture

    Colles' is classically an extra-articular fracture of the distal radius (within about 2.5 cm of the wrist) with dorsal displacement. An intra-articular fracture with carpal subluxation is a Barton's fracture.

    Upper limb deck · sprain vs strain

    Strains are injuries to muscle or the muscle–tendon junction, not to tendons alone. Sprains are ligament injuries.

    Upper limb deck · supracondylar fracture

    "Interior interosseous" should be the anterior interosseous nerve, the most commonly injured nerve in extension-type supracondylar fractures.

    Upper limb deck · deltoid

    Deltoid abducts mainly from about 15° to 90°. Above 90°, abduction needs upward rotation of the scapula by trapezius and serratus anterior.

    Lower limb decks · lateral ankle ligament

    Rolling your ankle is an inversion injury, not over-eversion. The lateral ligament has three parts: anterior talofibular (most often sprained), calcaneofibular and posterior talofibular. The deltoid ligament (medial) resists eversion.

    Lower limb decks · lumbar puncture landmark

    The ASIS is not the L4–5 landmark. The line joining the tops of the iliac crests (Tuffier's or the supracristal line) crosses about L4, and guides lumbar puncture at L3/4 or L4/5.

    UEAS innervation deck · femoral nerve roots

    The speaker notes give the femoral nerve as L3–L5. It is L2–L4, like the obturator nerve.

    UEAS anatomy deck · adductor magnus

    "The hamstring part of adductor longus" should read adductor magnus. Its hamstring part is supplied by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve.

    Lower limb deck · linea aspera

    The linea aspera gives attachment to the adductors and to vastus medialis, vastus lateralis and the short head of biceps femoris. The vasti are not adductors.

    Lower limb deck · venepuncture

    The great saphenous vein in front of the medial malleolus is used for emergency cut-down cannulation and coronary bypass grafts, not routine venepuncture. The "houseman's vein" of junior doctor folklore is the cephalic vein at the wrist.

    Lower limb deck · ankle and knee wording

    The ankle mortise is formed by the malleoli, not condyles. The "unhappy triad" causes pain, swelling and instability; loss of feeling in the knee is not a feature.

    Lower limb deck · superior gluteal nerve

    Its roots are L4–S1 (one slide says L5–S1).
